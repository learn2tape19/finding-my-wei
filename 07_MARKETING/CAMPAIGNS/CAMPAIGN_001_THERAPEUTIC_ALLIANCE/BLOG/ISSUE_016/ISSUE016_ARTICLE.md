# Issue 016 — Trust
## The Clinical Practice of Trust
**Status:** FOUNDER APPROVED — CANONICAL ARTICLE LOCKED (October 10, 2026)
**Publication:** The Tao of Clinical Touch
**Proposed week:** October 19–23, 2026

A patient once asked me a question midway through a session that I should have welcomed more quickly.

“How do you know that's the right place?”

I had a clinical explanation. I could describe the movement we were exploring, the symptoms they had reported, and why I had chosen that area. But the question wasn't only about anatomy. It was also about whether the person on the table could understand what I was doing—and whether they were allowed to challenge it.

I answered the question. Then I noticed something about the encounter: until that moment, I had been treating my reasoning as though it were already shared.

Trust can be mistaken for a patient's willingness to go along with us. They book the appointment, get on the table, and let us work. We may interpret that cooperation as confidence in our judgment. But agreeing to receive care is not the same as understanding every decision made during it.

In clinical practice, trust is less useful as a feeling we hope to inspire than as a relationship we have to keep making understandable.

### Explain the decision, not just the technique

A patient does not need a lecture in anatomy to participate in care. They do need a way to understand what we are trying to accomplish.

When I explain why I want to examine a movement or work in a particular area, I give the patient something they can respond to. They can tell me whether the explanation fits their experience. They can correct an assumption. They can ask what happens if we choose another approach.

This is not a performance of expertise. It is an invitation to examine the plan together.

There are times when I am uncertain. Saying so does not erase the value of my experience. It distinguishes what I observed from what I suspect, and what we know from what we still need to learn.

Trust has room for that distinction.

### Make your behavior predictable

The treatment room contains many small moments of uncertainty.

A patient may not know where my hands will move next, how much pressure I intend to use, how long a position will be held, or whether speaking up will interrupt the work. Those uncertainties may be minor to me and significant to them.

Predictability does not require narrating every movement. It means giving enough information that the next step is not an unwelcome surprise.

I can describe what I propose, check whether it is acceptable, and follow through on what we agreed. If I need to change the plan, I can say so before I change it.

Over time, consistency between what I say and what I do gives the patient a basis for deciding whether to rely on me. The point is not to appear perfectly certain. It is to be dependable when circumstances change.

### Leave room for disagreement

One of the clearest tests of a therapeutic relationship is what happens when the patient says no.

Perhaps they do not want a particular area treated. Perhaps an approach that made sense to me does not feel right to them. Perhaps they want to stop, ask a question, or pursue a different goal.

If I treat that response as resistance to overcome, I have changed the terms of the relationship. The patient is now being asked to defend a boundary that should already belong to them.

I can explain my reasoning, including any meaningful tradeoffs. I can offer alternatives. I can also accept that the patient may choose differently than I would.

A person's ability to disagree is not evidence that trust has failed. It may be evidence that the relationship allows honest participation.

### Respond when trust is tested

Even careful clinicians miss things.

I may misunderstand a goal, use language that confuses, or move faster than a patient expected. A patient may tell me that something feels uncomfortable or that I have not heard what they were trying to say.

The important question then is not whether I can defend my intention. It is whether I can recognize the effect and respond.

I can stop, acknowledge what I missed, ask what would be helpful, and change my behavior. An explanation may be useful, but it should not replace listening.

A therapeutic alliance does not depend on never making a mistake. It depends, in part, on whether a mistake can be named and addressed without making the patient responsible for protecting the clinician's confidence.

### Let trust remain the patient's decision

We sometimes speak of “building trust” as though it were a technique with a predictable result.

It isn't.

A clinician can act transparently, communicate clearly, respect boundaries, and remain accountable. Those actions create reasons a patient might choose to trust us. They do not entitle us to that trust.

And trust is not unlimited. A patient may trust my skill in one area and want more explanation in another. They may trust me today and reconsider tomorrow. They may value our work and still decide that another clinician is better suited to the next step.

That freedom matters.

The Tao of clinical touch is not an argument for passive care. It asks us to notice how much of the encounter depends on what we do before, during, and after an intervention.

Skill gives us something to offer.

Trust asks whether the person receiving it can see our reasoning, question our choices, and remain fully present in the decisions that concern their own body.
