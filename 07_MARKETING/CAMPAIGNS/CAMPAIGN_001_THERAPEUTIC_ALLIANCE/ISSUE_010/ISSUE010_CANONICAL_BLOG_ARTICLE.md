Last week, I didn't publish a blog.

That wasn't an oversight.

I was in Denver for the AMTA National Convention, and I made a conscious decision that my time was better spent preparing, presenting, listening, and being present with the people around me.

Coming home, I'm glad I did.

Because I don't think I could have written this week's piece before I went.

I arrived in Denver with nearly 30 years of clinical experience. I've spent decades with my hands on people. I've taught. I've presented. I've written a book. I've stood in front of rooms before.

I had every reason to feel confident.

And I did.

But confidence wasn't the only thing that came with me.

My nervous system came too.

### Knowing and Guarding Can Exist Together

There was a strange contradiction happening inside me.

I knew I belonged there.

At the same time, some part of me was still watching.

Guarding.

Waiting for the negative response.

Waiting for someone to challenge whether I belonged at the front of the room. Waiting to discover that perhaps other people didn't see me the way I had begun to see myself.

And then something very different happened.

People came up to meet me.

Some told me they had been following my work. Some wanted to talk about *The Tao of Clinical Touch*. Some wanted a book signed.

And I found myself working alongside younger, passionate educators who brought their own ideas, experience, questions, and energy into the room.

A few referred to me as a mentor.

Others told me they were honored to meet me.

I'll admit it.

That felt strange.

But it also made me proud.

Not proud because I had somehow arrived at the place where I had all the answers.

Almost the opposite.

I was beginning to recognize how much I have to contribute **and how much I still have to learn.**

More than one thing can be true.

### Sometimes We Need to Zoom Out

I've spent much of this year writing and talking about what happens when the nervous system doesn't immediately interpret an experience as safe.

We guard.

We predict.

We prepare.

We use previous experience to anticipate what might happen next.

Those responses make sense clinically.

Apparently, they make sense standing in front of a classroom in Denver too.

What surprised me was realizing that some of the very ideas I have been asking clinicians to consider were helping me understand my own experience.

Instead of judging the nervousness, I could notice it.

Instead of treating it as evidence that I didn't belong, I could zoom out.

Thirty years of experience didn't disappear because part of me was nervous.

Confidence and vulnerability could exist at the same time.

So could teacher and student.

So could mentor and colleague.

**More than one thing can be true.**

And when I stopped requiring one of those truths to defeat the others, something opened.

### Leave Room for What You Didn't Expect

I went to Denver prepared to teach.

I expected to help.

I expected conversations with colleagues and therapists.

I expected to talk about the book.

I even brought an invention I've been quietly developing because I thought the convention might be an opportunity to begin showing it to people.

I had a plan.

What I couldn't plan was how the experience would feel.

I couldn't plan someone telling me I had influenced the way they practice.

I couldn't plan younger educators treating my experience as something valuable while simultaneously reminding me how much their curiosity and enthusiasm could teach me.

I couldn't plan the feeling of realizing that perhaps my role in this profession is changing.

That doesn't mean leaving clinical practice behind.

It means recognizing that experience creates another responsibility:

**to share it.**

And sharing doesn't mean standing at the front of the room delivering answers.

Sometimes it means entering a room with everything you've learned and remaining open enough to discover what everyone else brought with them.

A plan should organize the encounter.

It shouldn't seal it.

### The Outcome May Not Look Like the Plan

That's true in treatment.

It turns out it's true in life as well.

We often decide what success should look like before an experience begins.

Then we measure ourselves against that prediction.

But some of the most meaningful outcomes in Denver weren't things I would have thought to measure.

A conversation in the hallway.

Watching someone else's teaching style.

A therapist holding my book.

A younger educator calling me a mentor.

The realization that being respected doesn't require pretending I have nothing left to learn.

None of those were on my schedule.

They may have been among the most important things that happened.

### Possibility Is the Practice

Over the past several weeks, I've been exploring a progression:

**Understanding → Preparation → Permission → Listening → Timing → Possibility**

I originally thought about that sequence through the lens of clinical encounters.

Denver reminded me that these aren't principles we apply only to the people on our tables.

We live inside them too.

Possibility isn't optimism.

It isn't believing everything will work out.

And it certainly isn't the absence of nervousness or uncertainty.

Possibility is what appears when we stop demanding that an experience confirm the story we carried into it.

I arrived in Denver with 30 years of experience.

I came home with 30 years of experience **and more questions.**

More people I want to learn from.

More people I want to teach.

More confidence in what I have to offer.

And perhaps a little more willingness to allow myself to feel proud when someone tells me it mattered.

I didn't expect all of that.

Fortunately, I left enough room for it to happen.

**Possibility is what becomes available when we stop needing to be right.**
