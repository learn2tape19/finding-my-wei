### Caption

We gather information before treatment for a reason.

History. Movement. Palpation. Conversation. What the person expects. What they are worried about. What has helped before. What has not.

Then treatment begins, and more information arrives.

A movement changes. A familiar symptom behaves differently. The tissue response is not what we expected. The client describes a sensation that reframes what we thought we understood.

That information should matter.

A plan is useful because it organizes what we know. Clinical reasoning stays useful because it can reorganize when what we know changes.

The goal is not to abandon direction every time something unexpected happens. The goal is to avoid becoming so attached to the original direction that we stop noticing what the encounter is teaching us.

New information should change something.

Sometimes the technique changes. Sometimes the pressure changes. Sometimes the goal changes. Sometimes the most important change is simply the question we are asking.

What information would have to appear before you changed your plan?
