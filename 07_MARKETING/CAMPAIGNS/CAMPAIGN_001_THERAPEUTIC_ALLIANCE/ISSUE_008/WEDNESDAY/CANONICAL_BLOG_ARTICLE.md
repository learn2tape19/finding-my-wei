# Changing Course Is Clinical Reasoning

**Issue 008: The Plan Must Be Allowed to Change**

There is a quiet pressure in clinical work to be right early.

A client describes what is happening. We ask questions. We observe movement. We palpate. We begin connecting information. Before long, an explanation starts to form.

That is useful. Clinical work needs direction.

The problem begins when direction quietly becomes obligation.

Once we have explained what we think is happening, chosen a treatment strategy, and begun working, it can become surprisingly difficult to let the encounter change our mind.

Not because we are careless.

Often because we are trying to be competent.

We want our reasoning to hold together. We want the plan to make sense. We want the session to feel coherent.

But coherence is not the same as accuracy.

## The first plan is built from incomplete information

Every treatment begins with a limitation that cannot be removed: we do not yet know everything that will happen during the encounter.

We know what the person has told us. We know what we observed before treatment. We know what our assessment suggested. We know what our experience tells us is plausible.

That is enough to form a working hypothesis.

It is not enough to stop listening.

Once treatment begins, the person continues to provide information.

A movement changes after an intervention. A symptom becomes clearer. A sensation appears in a position that seemed comfortable moments earlier. Pressure that was initially welcome becomes too much. A technique produces a response that does not match what we expected.

None of that information is an interruption to the plan.

It is part of the plan.

## Clinical reasoning continues after the decision

We sometimes talk about clinical reasoning as though it happens before treatment.

Assess. Decide. Treat.

But experienced clinical work is rarely that linear.

A more useful description may be:

Observe. Form a hypothesis. Act carefully. Observe again. Compare the response with what you expected. Reconsider what you think you know.

Then continue.

The reasoning does not end when the first technique begins.

It becomes more informed.

That distinction changes the meaning of revision.

If the purpose of the session is to prove the original hypothesis, changing course can feel like failure.

If the purpose is to understand and help the person in front of us, changing course can be evidence that attention is still active.

## The person is not required to confirm our explanation

Clinical experience gives us patterns.

Patterns are useful because they help us recognize possibilities quickly. They can make assessment more efficient and help us choose reasonable starting points.

But a pattern is not the person.

The moment we become more interested in preserving the pattern than understanding the response, the hierarchy changes.

Now the person is being asked to fit the explanation.

That is where a useful hypothesis can become a constraint.

The better question is not, "How do I make this treatment work?"

It may be, "What is this response asking me to reconsider?"

Sometimes the answer is very little.

Change the pressure. Adjust the position. Slow the pace. Ask another question.

Sometimes the answer is larger.

Change the technique. Change the goal. Reconsider the assumption that organized the session in the first place.

The size of the revision matters less than our willingness to notice when revision is needed.

## Precision can include changing your mind

We often associate precision with consistency.

Do the right thing accurately and repeatably.

There is value in that. Skill matters. Technique matters. Knowledge matters.

But clinical precision also depends on responsiveness.

The right intervention at the beginning of an encounter may not remain the right intervention twenty minutes later.

The person has changed because the encounter has changed.

New information exists now that did not exist when the plan was formed.

Precision means allowing that information to matter.

This does not require abandoning structure or chasing every new sensation. It requires something quieter.

Availability.

The willingness to remain close enough to the encounter that the next piece of information can still influence what happens next.

## A working hypothesis should remain workable

There is a reason I like the language of a working hypothesis.

It gives us permission to be thoughtful without pretending to be certain.

It allows us to bring our training, experience, and clinical judgment into the room while leaving space for the person to teach us something we did not know at the beginning.

That is not indecision.

It is disciplined curiosity.

We begin somewhere.

We pay attention.

We let the response become information.

And when the information changes, we remain willing to change with it.

Perhaps one of the clearest signs of clinical reasoning is not how confidently we can explain the first plan.

It is how thoughtfully we can recognize when the person in front of us is asking for a better one.

## Reflection

Think about a recent session that did not unfold the way you expected.

When did you first notice the plan needed to change?

And what helped you trust that changing course was not leaving the reasoning behind, but continuing it?
