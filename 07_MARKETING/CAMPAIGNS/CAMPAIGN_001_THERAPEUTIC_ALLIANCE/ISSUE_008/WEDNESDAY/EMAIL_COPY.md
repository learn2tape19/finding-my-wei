**CHANGING COURSE IS CLINICAL REASONING.**

## Body

There is a quiet pressure in clinical work to be right early.

We assess. We form an explanation. We choose a direction. Then treatment begins.

But the person keeps giving us information.

A movement changes. A symptom behaves differently. Pressure that was welcome becomes too much. A response does not match what we expected.

That information is not an interruption to clinical reasoning.

It is part of it.

A plan gives us somewhere to begin. A working hypothesis gives us a way to organize what we know. Neither should require the person to keep confirming our first idea.

Clinical reasoning continues after the first decision.

We act carefully. We observe. We compare the response with what we expected. Then we decide what the new information asks of us.

Sometimes that confirms the plan.

Sometimes it changes it.

Changing course is not necessarily evidence that the reasoning failed. It may be evidence that attention is still active.

This week's article explores why responsiveness belongs inside precision, and why a working hypothesis should remain workable.
