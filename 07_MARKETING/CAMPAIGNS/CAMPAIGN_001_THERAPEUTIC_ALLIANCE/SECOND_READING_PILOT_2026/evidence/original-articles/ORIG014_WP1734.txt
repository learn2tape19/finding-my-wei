  Words can protect regulation or reintroduce threat.  



 We spend years learning what to do with our hands. 



 How to assess. How to palpate. How to choose an intervention. How to recognize when something changes. 



 We spend considerably less time learning what to say about what we find. 



 And yet the moment we give an experience a name, offer an explanation, or tell someone what we think is happening, we have introduced another clinical input. 



 Language. 



 The treatment does not end when our hands leave the body. 



 Sometimes, what we say next matters just as much. 



 What You Say Becomes Part of the Treatment 



 A client sits up after the session and asks the familiar question: 



  What did you find?  



 It is tempting to answer with certainty. 



 “Your hip is out.” 



 “This muscle is completely locked up.” 



 “You have a lot of scar tissue.” 



 “Your posture is causing this.” 



 These explanations can sound clinical. They can also become stories the client carries home. 



 A sensation that was simply uncomfortable now has a cause. 



 A movement that felt uncertain now has something supposedly wrong with it. 



 A body that hurt now has a problem to monitor. 



 Our words have changed the context in which the next sensation will be experienced. 



 That deserves our attention. 



 Expectation Changes Experience 



 Expectation is not imaginary. 



 What someone anticipates can influence how they attend to an experience, interpret it, and respond to it. 



 Tell someone, “This is probably going to hurt,” and you have given their nervous system information before the stimulus even arrives. 



 Tell them, “You may notice some sensation here. Let me know what you experience,” and you have done something different. 



 You have left room for discovery. 



 The difference may appear subtle. 



 Clinically, it is enormous. 



 One statement predicts an experience. 



 The other permits one. 



 This doesn’t mean withholding information or pretending everything will feel good. It means recognizing that our language participates in expectation. 



 And expectation enters the room before our hands do. 



 Do Not Give Pain a Story It Didn’t Ask For 



 People experiencing pain naturally want explanations. 



 We naturally want to provide them. 



 The danger begins when explanation becomes certainty. 



 We feel tension and call it dysfunction. 



 We notice asymmetry and call it imbalance. 



 We find tenderness and construct a mechanical story around it. 



 Perhaps that story is correct. 



 Perhaps it isn’t. 



 But once spoken with authority, it can become difficult for the client to separate what they actually experience from what we told them their experience means. 



 Clinical reasoning should help us investigate possibilities, not require the client to inherit our conclusions. 



 There is nothing clinically incomplete about acknowledging what we don’t yet know. Uncertainty can remain part of the assessment without becoming something we need to resolve for the client before we have enough information. 



 Recommend Without Creating Dependence 



 The same discipline matters when we discuss what comes next. 



 “You need to come back every week.” 



 “You’ll tighten right back up if we don’t stay on this.” 



 “This is something we’ll have to keep fixing.” 



 Listen to the relationship those sentences create. 



 The clinician becomes necessary. 



 The body becomes unreliable. 



 Care becomes something delivered to the client rather than something developed with them. 



 There may absolutely be reasons to recommend another session. 



 Explain what you observed. Describe what you would like to reassess. Offer a reasonable path forward. 



 But recommendation and dependence are not the same thing. 



 Good clinical communication helps people understand their options without convincing them that progress depends upon continued access to us. 



 Give Them Language They Can Leave With 



 Eventually, the client walks out the door. 



 What travels with them? 



 Perhaps soreness. 



 Perhaps relief. 



 Perhaps greater movement. 



 But also language. 



 The language we used to describe their body. 



 The language we used to explain their pain. 



 The language we used to frame what changed. 



 That language may influence what they notice tomorrow. 



 So give them words that preserve possibility. 



 “You moved differently afterward.” 



 “Let’s see how that feels over the next couple of days.” 



 “You don’t need to protect it just because you noticed it.” 



 “If something changes, that’s information. We can learn from it.” 



 We cannot control what happens after a treatment. 



 Nor should we try. 



 Our job is not to provide a story so convincing that the client cannot imagine another one. 



 It is to help create conditions in which they can continue discovering what their body is capable of. 



 Our hands may begin that conversation. 



 Our language helps determine whether it remains open.
