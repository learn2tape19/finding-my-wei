Title: The Decision Before the Decision

 Before a practitioner chooses a technique, applies pressure, or explains a treatment plan, another decision has already been made. 



 It is quieter than the intervention that follows, but often more influential. 



 The practitioner has decided what they believe is happening. 



 That belief may be thoughtful and well supported. It may also be shaped by habit, expectation, training, or the understandable desire to solve the problem quickly. 



 Either way, it becomes the lens through which everything else is interpreted. 



 We Do Not Assess Without a Lens 



 Clinical assessment is often presented as if it were a neutral act: observe, palpate, test, interpret, treat. 



 But we do not enter an assessment empty-handed. We bring our education, previous successes, preferred methods, assumptions, and expectations with us. 



 These filters help us organize information. They allow experienced practitioners to recognize patterns efficiently. 



 They can also narrow what we are able to see. 



 When we become convinced too early that we understand the problem, we may stop listening for information that challenges our first impression. We begin gathering evidence to support a conclusion rather than remaining open to what the client is showing us. 



 The assessment may continue, but discovery has already ended. 



 Information Is Not Yet Understanding 



 A client can provide a great deal of information: where it hurts, when it started, what aggravates it, and what has already been tried. 



 Our hands provide more: temperature, tone, movement, resistance, guarding, and response. 



 But information alone does not tell us what it means. 



 The same muscular tension might reflect load, compensation, apprehension, fatigue, unfamiliarity, or protection. The same limitation in movement may mean something different depending on the person, the environment, and the level of trust present during the assessment. 



 Clinical understanding develops when we place information in context. 



 That requires more than collecting findings. It requires listening closely enough to notice how those findings relate to the person in front of us. 



 The Nervous System Participates in the Examination 



 Assessment is not something we simply perform on a passive body. 



 The client is responding to us while we assess them. 



 Our pace, language, confidence, touch, and expectations all influence what becomes available. A person who feels hurried, judged, or uncertain may present differently from someone who feels heard and included. 



 Guarding can increase. Movement can become less natural. The client may offer the answer they believe we want instead of the experience they are actually having. 



 This does not make the assessment unreliable. It makes the relationship part of the assessment. 



 The state of the nervous system is not interference with the clinical picture. It is part of the clinical picture. 



 Curiosity Protects Clinical Reasoning 



 Expertise is not the absence of uncertainty. 



 It is the ability to remain curious long enough for understanding to develop. 



 Curiosity allows us to form a hypothesis without becoming trapped by it. It gives the client room to correct us. It helps us notice when their response does not match our expectation. 



 Instead of asking only, “What technique does this finding indicate?” we can ask: 



 “What else might explain what I am noticing?” 



 “What changes when the client feels more supported?” 



 “What am I assuming—and have I verified it?” 



 These questions do not weaken clinical confidence. They make it more trustworthy. 



 Better Decisions Begin With Better Understanding 



 Technique matters. Precision matters. Experience matters. 



 But each becomes more useful when guided by an understanding that has not been rushed. 



 The decision before the decision is the meaning we give to what we see, hear, and feel. 



 If that meaning is incomplete, even an expertly delivered intervention may address the wrong problem. 



 Pause before deciding what the client needs. 



 Listen beyond the first answer. 



 Let the person—not only the pattern—shape what comes next. 



 Clinical understanding changes clinical decisions.
