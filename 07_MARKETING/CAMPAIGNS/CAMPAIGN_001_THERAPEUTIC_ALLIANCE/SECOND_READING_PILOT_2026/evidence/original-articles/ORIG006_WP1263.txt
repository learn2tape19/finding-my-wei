Title: The Work Before the Work

 Every treatment begins before contact. 



 Before assessment. Before palpation. Before technique. 



 Long before our hands arrive, another conversation is already unfolding. 



 It begins with preparation. 



 Not simply whether the table is made or the room is clean. Preparation includes the conditions we create before another person enters our care: the environment, our attention, our pace, and our presence. 



 None of these replace clinical skill. 



 They shape the conditions in which clinical skill can be received. 



 A client does not arrive as a collection of tissues waiting to be assessed. They arrive already experiencing the room, the interaction, and us. They are noticing what feels predictable and what does not. They are gathering information before we have asked the first question. 



 So are we. 



 That makes the moments before touch clinically meaningful. 



 There is work in preparing the room so it feels intentional rather than hurried. There is work in arriving ourselves before asking someone else to settle. There is work in resisting the urge to move immediately toward explanation, assessment, or intervention simply because those are the parts of care we have been trained to recognize as productive. 



 Sometimes preparation is quieter than that. 



 It may be the pace of the greeting. The space we leave for an answer. The way we listen without already preparing the next question. The willingness to let the encounter begin before deciding where it needs to go. 



 This is not customer service draped in clinical language. 



 It is part of the therapeutic alliance. 



 Technique matters. Assessment matters. Clinical reasoning matters. But each enters a relationship that has already begun forming. 



 The work before the work is not another technique to master. 



 It is preparation for everything that follows. 



 The work begins before touch. 



 Not with pressure. 



 With preparation.
