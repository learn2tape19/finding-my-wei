Explain decisions. Leave room for disagreement. Let trust remain the patient’s decision.

ISSUE No. 016 · TRUST

A patient once asked me a question midway through a session that I should have welcomed more quickly.

“How do you know that's the right place?”

I had a clinical explanation. I could describe the movement we were exploring, the symptoms they had reported, and why I had chosen that area. But the question wasn't only about anatomy. It was also about whether the person on the table could understand what I was doing—and whether they were allowed to challenge it.

I answered the question. Then I noticed something about the encounter: until that moment, I had been treating my reasoning as though it were already shared.

Trust can be mistaken for a patient's willingness to go along with us. They book the appointment, get on the table, and let us work. We may interpret that cooperation as confidence in our judgment. But agreeing to receive care is not the same as understanding every decision made during it.

In clinical practice, trust is less useful as a feeling we hope to inspire than as a relationship we have to keep making understandable.
