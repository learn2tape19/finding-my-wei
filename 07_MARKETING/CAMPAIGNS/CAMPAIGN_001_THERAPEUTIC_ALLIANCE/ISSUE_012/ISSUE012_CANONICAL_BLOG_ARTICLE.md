# The Clinical Practice of Agency

Range improves.

Guarding decreases.

Movement becomes easier.

We feel the change beneath our hands. We see it when we reassess. We compare where someone started with where they finished, and because it happened while we were working with them, it is easy to believe the change belongs to us.

It does not.

We may have participated in it. Our hands, reasoning, communication, pacing, pressure, timing, and restraint may have helped create the conditions in which change became possible.

But the person still has to decide whether that change is safe enough to keep.

Agency begins there.

## The Change Is Not Yours

Practitioner participation is not the same as ownership.

We can guide movement. We can alter the environment. We can offer information, support, reassurance, challenge, and possibility.

But we cannot make another nervous system trust the result.

At some point, the person has to experience the change as something available to them rather than something produced by us.

That distinction does not diminish clinical work. It makes the work more precise.

You can create the conditions. The body still has to choose.

## Better on the Table Is Not the Finish Line

The shoulder moves farther.

Pain decreases.

Tone softens.

The before-and-after comparison looks convincing. Something changed.

But what happens when your hands are no longer guiding the movement?

A person may comfortably allow a movement passively and still hesitate when asked to initiate it themselves. That does not make the improvement false. It tells us there is another question to ask:

What does this person trust themselves to do?

Because the movement that matters is not confined to a treatment table.

It is reaching into the cabinet tomorrow morning. Putting on a shirt. Lifting a child. Returning to training. Moving without first checking whether it is going to hurt.

Change observed is not always change owned.

## Let Them Try

There comes a point when we need to remove our hands.

Then ask:

*Try that again.*

Now we are learning something different.

Does the person initiate easily?

Do they hesitate?

Brace?

Hold their breath?

Does the movement that appeared under our guidance remain available when they are responsible for producing it?

We are not simply testing whether the treatment worked. We are giving the person an opportunity to experience the movement as their own.

If the movement becomes smaller, that is not failure. If uncertainty returns, that is not failure either.

It is information.

Agency cannot be demonstrated by the practitioner.

At some point, your hands have to leave.

## Know When to Step Back

Clinical skill is often measured by what we know how to do.

There is another skill that matters just as much:

Knowing when to stop doing it.

Move here. Relax this. Try again. Let me help.

Each may be useful at the right moment. But if every successful movement continues to require our hands, our reassurance, or our instruction, where does the confidence live?

Still with us.

There comes a point when guidance has done its job.

Remove the hands. Allow exploration. Let hesitation occur without immediately correcting it. Watch what the person discovers.

Stepping back is not abandonment. And doing less is not automatically better.

The question is whether our continued involvement is still helping someone discover their capacity or beginning to replace their experience of it.

Guidance creates possibility. Space allows ownership.

## They Have to Own the Change

Years ago, I worked with a patient whose shoulder had become increasingly restricted.

We made progress. Range improved. Pain decreased. Movement became easier.

Then I went away on vacation.

While I was gone, she left me a voicemail.

She had clasped her bra behind her back for the first time in years.

But that was not the part that stayed with me.

She had not prepared for the movement. She had not tested the shoulder first. She had not waited for me to tell her it was safe.

She simply moved.

At the time, I thought the breakthrough had happened during treatment.

I understand it differently now.

The important moment happened when I was not there.

Her nervous system no longer needed my hands to demonstrate the possibility. She trusted the movement enough to initiate it herself.

That is agency.

Our work takes place in relationship. But the relationship is not meant to make the practitioner indispensable.

Sometimes the best evidence of our work happens when we are not there to see it.

Agency is what remains when the practitioner steps away.
