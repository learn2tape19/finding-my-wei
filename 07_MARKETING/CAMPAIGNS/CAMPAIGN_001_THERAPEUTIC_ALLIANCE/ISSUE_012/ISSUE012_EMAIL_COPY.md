**Subject:** When does the change become theirs?

**Preheader:** Better movement is one thing. Owning it is another.

## THE CLINICAL PRACTICE OF AGENCY

There is a moment in treatment that can be easy to miss.

Range improves. Guarding decreases. Movement becomes easier.

We feel it beneath our hands. We see it when we reassess. And because the change happened while we were working with someone, it is tempting to believe we created it.

We participated in it.

That is different.

Our hands, reasoning, communication, pacing, pressure, timing, and restraint can help create the conditions in which change becomes possible.

But eventually our hands have to leave.

Then a different question becomes important:

**What does this person trust themselves to do without me?**

Years ago, a patient gave me an answer to that question while I was away on vacation.

We had been working with a shoulder that had become increasingly restricted. We had made progress. Then, while I was gone, she left me a voicemail.

She had clasped her bra behind her back for the first time in years.

What stayed with me wasn't simply that she could do it.

She hadn't prepared for the movement. She hadn't tested the shoulder. She hadn't waited for me to tell her it was safe.

**She simply moved.**

At the time, I thought the breakthrough had happened during treatment.

I understand it differently now.

The important moment happened when I wasn't there.

This week's *Tao of Clinical Touch* explores that transition: from change we can observe to change the person actually owns.

**The goal of treatment is not compliance. It is agency.**
