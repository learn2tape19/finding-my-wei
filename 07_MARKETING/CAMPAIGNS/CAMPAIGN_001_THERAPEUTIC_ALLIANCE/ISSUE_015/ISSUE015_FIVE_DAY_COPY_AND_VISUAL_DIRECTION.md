## Monday — THE INTERVENTION NEEDS A REASON
**Focus:** Indication. **Narrative:** A technique in the repertoire is not an indication.

**Feed / Landscape**  
**Headline:** THE INTERVENTION NEEDS A REASON  
**Support:** A technique is an option. The patient's goal gives it a purpose.  
**Closing:** Before the next move, know what you are asking it to accomplish.

**Story 1 — Option versus indication**  
Headline: JUST BECAUSE WE CAN DOESN'T MEAN WE SHOULD.  
Copy: A growing repertoire gives us more choices. It does not tell us which one this person needs.

**Story 2 — The clinical question**  
Headline: WHAT ARE WE TRYING TO CHANGE?  
Copy: Start with the concern they brought in. Identify the purpose before selecting the intervention.

**Story 3 — A reason to begin**  
Headline: PURPOSE BEFORE TECHNIQUE.  
Copy: A clear reason won't guarantee the result. It gives us a basis for deciding what comes next.

**Proposed visual narrative:** Feed: clinician and patient conversing beside the treatment table, patient naturally demonstrating a meaningful arm movement; no generic clipboard pose. Landscape: wider version of the same clinical decision point, composed independently. Stories: (1) folded treatment towel and therapist's hands at rest while listening, (2) patient demonstrating the relevant movement, (3) clinician and patient making eye contact before care begins.

## Tuesday — THE DOSE IS A CLINICAL DECISION
**Focus:** Calibration. **Narrative:** Pressure, pace and duration are adjustable inputs.

**Feed / Landscape**  
**Headline:** THE DOSE IS A CLINICAL DECISION  
**Support:** Pressure is one variable. Pace, duration, and complexity matter too.  
**Closing:** Match the input to the person—not the person to the technique.

**Story 1 — Multiple variables**  
Headline: DOSE IS MORE THAN PRESSURE.  
Copy: Pace. Duration. Repetition. Complexity. Each is a decision we can adjust.

**Story 2 — Patient-specific calibration**  
Headline: WHAT IS APPROPRIATE TODAY?  
Copy: The useful amount depends on the person, their goal, and how they respond.

**Story 3 — Precision, not performance**  
Headline: INTENSITY ISN'T THE OBJECTIVE.  
Copy: The objective is a useful intervention, negotiated and calibrated together.

**Proposed visual narrative:** Feed: realistic close clinical view of therapist applying measured contact while checking in with an alert patient; no exaggerated pressure. Landscape: wider environmental angle. Stories: (1) therapist preparing contact, (2) patient's visible, natural feedback during check-in, (3) therapist adjusting position or pressure without dramatization.

## Wednesday — REASSESS BEFORE YOU ADD MORE
**Focus:** Reassessment. **Narrative:** A change is information, not automatic permission to intensify.

**Feed / Landscape**  
**Headline:** REASSESS BEFORE YOU ADD MORE  
**Support:** A change is information, not an instruction.  
**Closing:** Return to the goal before deciding what comes next.

**Story 1 — Interrupt the automatic next step**  
Headline: SOMETHING CHANGED.  
Copy: That is the moment to check—not automatically the moment to do more.

**Story 2 — Return to the agreed purpose**  
Headline: DOES THE GOAL LOOK DIFFERENT?  
Copy: Ask what they notice. Revisit the movement or activity you agreed to explore.

**Story 3 — Let the response inform the plan**  
Headline: INFORMATION BEFORE ESCALATION.  
Copy: The next step needs a reason grounded in what you have learned.

**Proposed visual narrative:** Feed: patient actively reassessing the original functional movement beside table with therapist observing rather than touching. Landscape: separate wider frame with enough negative space for typography. Stories: (1) therapist pauses with hands away, (2) patient repeats movement, (3) clinician and patient briefly discuss what changed.

## Thursday — CHANGE THE PLAN, NOT THE PATIENT
**Focus:** Decision. **Narrative:** Clinical confidence includes revising the initial approach.

**Feed / Landscape**  
**Headline:** CHANGE THE PLAN, NOT THE PATIENT  
**Support:** A plan should respond to the person—not demand that the person justify it.  
**Closing:** Adapting is a clinical decision, not a retreat.

**Story 1 — Notice investment in the plan**  
Headline: WHEN THE PLAN STOPS FITTING...  
Copy: Notice the temptation to push ahead simply because you started.

**Story 2 — Respond instead of persuade**  
Headline: LISTEN TO THE RESPONSE.  
Copy: Adjust the input. Reconsider the goal. Ask a better question.

**Story 3 — Authority without insistence**  
Headline: CONFIDENCE CAN CHANGE COURSE.  
Copy: Good judgment includes modifying, stopping, or referring when appropriate.

**Proposed visual narrative:** Feed: clinician and patient discussing an adjusted plan, therapist open-handed and engaged; no staged conflict. Landscape: separate composition emphasizing collaboration. Stories: (1) therapist pauses mid-plan, (2) patient indicates a preference or different movement, (3) therapist changes setup or position in response.

## Friday — KNOW WHEN THE WORK IS COMPLETE
**Focus:** Completion. **Narrative:** Continuing requires as much justification as beginning.

**Feed / Landscape**  
**Headline:** KNOW WHEN THE WORK IS COMPLETE  
**Support:** More intervention is not automatically more care.  
**Closing:** Knowing when to put our hands down is part of knowing how to use them.

**Story 1 — Completion is a decision**  
Headline: A SESSION HAS AN END TIME.  
Copy: Useful intervention may have an earlier one.

**Story 2 — Ask what continuing adds**  
Headline: WHAT WOULD MORE ACCOMPLISH?  
Copy: If further input lacks a clear purpose, stopping deserves consideration.

**Story 3 — The clinical close**  
Headline: KNOW WHEN TO PUT YOUR HANDS DOWN.  
Copy: Explain what you observed. Respect what remains uncertain. Leave the next choices with the person.

**Proposed visual narrative:** Feed: therapist and patient at natural session close, patient comfortably standing or preparing to leave; therapist no longer treating. Landscape: independent wide clinical room composition, human presence without staged triumph. Stories: (1) hands leave the treatment position, (2) therapist and patient reflect on the agreed goal, (3) patient exits into an ordinary day, without visual implication of miraculous recovery.
