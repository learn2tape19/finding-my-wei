# Issue 015 — Restraint
## The Clinical Practice of Restraint
**Status:** DRAFT — FOUNDER REVIEW REQUIRED  
**Source:** Founder-approved Issue 015 editorial brief, October 2, 2026  
**Publication:** The Tao of Clinical Touch

There is a moment in almost every treatment when the clinician has a choice.

The technique is working—or at least something is happening. The patient has given us information. We could continue, increase the pressure, move to the next structure, or introduce another approach. We could also stop long enough to ask whether the next intervention has earned its place.

Experience does not eliminate that decision. It makes us more accountable for it.

We accumulate techniques because we want to help. Over time, a larger repertoire can make it easier to find something to do and harder to notice when doing something else is unnecessary. A treatment plan can quietly become a sequence we feel obliged to finish. But the person on the table did not come to experience the full range of our skills. They came with a concern, a goal, and a nervous system responding to the encounter in real time.

Clinical restraint begins there. Not with the instruction to do less, but with the discipline to make each intervention answer to a purpose.

### The intervention needs a reason

Before I choose a technique, I need to know what I am asking it to accomplish.

That purpose might be to explore a movement that has become uncomfortable, make a familiar activity easier, or help someone tolerate touch in an area that has felt threatening. The purpose should make sense to the patient as well as to me. It should also leave room for the possibility that my first idea is wrong.

Without a reason, the treatment risks becoming a tour of findings. One restricted area leads to another. Every new observation becomes an invitation to intervene. The session fills with activity, but the relationship between that activity and the patient's priorities grows less clear.

A clear indication does not guarantee a result. It gives us something more valuable: a reason to begin and a basis for deciding whether to continue.

### The dose is a clinical decision

Once an intervention has a purpose, we still have to choose its dose.

In hands-on care, dose is not only pressure. It includes pace, duration, repetition, the number of areas addressed, the complexity of the encounter, and the amount of new information we ask someone to process. These variables are not badges of effort. They are clinical decisions.

A patient may welcome firm contact and respond well to it. Another may prefer a lighter approach, or need time to establish what feels tolerable. Neither response makes one style universally correct. The question is whether the input is appropriate for this person, in this moment, in service of the agreed goal.

When I match the intervention to the response rather than asking the response to justify my preferred intervention, restraint becomes a form of precision.

That does not mean avoiding challenge. Sometimes the work calls for a carefully negotiated challenge. The distinction is whether we are testing a useful possibility together or simply adding intensity because we can.

### Reassess before you add more

The next decision is often the most easily skipped.

Something feels different under my hands. The patient reports a change. Perhaps movement is easier; perhaps it is not. The temptation is to interpret any change as permission to continue—or to add something else in pursuit of a more impressive result.

But a change is information, not an instruction.

Reassessment brings us back to the original purpose. What does the patient notice? Has the movement or activity we agreed to explore changed in a meaningful way? Has the intervention introduced discomfort, fatigue, uncertainty, or an unexpected response? Does the next step still have a reason?

Not every response can be explained in the moment, and not every useful observation proves a particular physiological mechanism. We can be attentive without pretending certainty.

A plan that cannot change in response to new information is no longer a clinical plan. It is a performance.

### Change the plan, not the patient

There is a subtle pressure that can enter a treatment when the clinician becomes invested in an approach.

We explain why it should work. We ask for a little more time. We encourage the patient to tolerate a little more intensity. Sometimes those choices are appropriate and mutually agreed upon. Sometimes they are signs that we are defending the plan instead of listening to the person.

Restraint asks us to notice the difference.

If the intervention is not serving its purpose, changing course is not a failure of confidence. It may be the clearest expression of it. We can adjust the input, reconsider the goal, ask a better question, or acknowledge that the next useful step lies beyond what this session—or our scope of practice—can provide.

Clinical authority is not the power to make every intervention work. It is the responsibility to make decisions that remain accountable to the person receiving care.

### Know when the work is complete

A session has a scheduled ending. Useful intervention may have an earlier one.

Perhaps the agreed movement is easier and further work offers no clear advantage. Perhaps the patient has reached the amount of input they want today. Perhaps the response suggests that another approach, more time, or a referral would be more appropriate. In each case, the decision to stop deserves as much thought as the decision to begin.

This is not an argument for shorter sessions, lighter treatment, or doing the minimum. It is an argument against mistaking more intervention for more care.

Completion also changes how we close. We can describe what we observed, acknowledge uncertainty where it exists, and help the patient understand what to pay attention to without turning every sensation into a problem to monitor. We do not need to claim that we fixed them. We need to leave them with a clear understanding of what we did, why we stopped, and what choices remain available.

The Tao often points toward action without imposition. In clinical practice, that is not a mystical instruction to stand aside. It is the difficult, ordinary work of remaining responsive when our training, our habits, and even our desire to help urge us to do more.

Knowing a hundred techniques may expand our options.

Knowing why to choose one, when to change it, and when to put our hands down is what makes those options useful.
