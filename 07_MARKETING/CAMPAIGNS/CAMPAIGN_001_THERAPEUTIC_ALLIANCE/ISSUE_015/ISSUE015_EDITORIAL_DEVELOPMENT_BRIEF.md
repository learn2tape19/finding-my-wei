## Editorial Purpose

Issue 014 examined how clinical language shapes expectation during and beyond the encounter. Issue 015 turns from what clinicians say to the decisions they make while treatment unfolds.

Restraint is not simply doing less. It is the practiced ability to distinguish useful intervention from habitual intervention, modify a plan when the person responds differently than expected, and recognize when additional input no longer serves the clinical purpose.

The distinguishing subject is **clinical judgment**: decisions informed by the person in front of us, not the clinician's need to complete a technique or demonstrate expertise.

Avoid replaying the broad principles of permission, waiting, agency, or dependence already developed in earlier issues. Those are foundations, not this issue's destination.

## Central Proposition — LOCKED

**Clinical maturity includes knowing when to intervene, when to adapt, and when to stop.**

Restraint is an active expression of judgment. It does not require withholding indicated care, ignoring patient preferences, or treating minimal intervention as inherently superior.

## Core Question — LOCKED

**How does an experienced clinician determine when intervention is necessary, when to modify it, and when to stop?**

## Weekly Editorial Movement — LOCKED

**Indication → Calibration → Reassessment → Decision → Completion**

### Monday — THE INTERVENTION NEEDS A REASON
**Focus: Indication**

The presence of a technique in our repertoire is not, by itself, a reason to use it. Before intervening, identify the clinical purpose, the patient's priorities, and the response that would make the intervention worth continuing.

**Distinctive question:** What am I trying to accomplish, and whose goal does it serve?

### Tuesday — THE DOSE IS A CLINICAL DECISION
**Focus: Calibration**

Pressure, duration, pace, and complexity are adjustable inputs—not demonstrations of commitment. The appropriate amount is contingent on the person, context, goals, and response.

**Distinctive question:** What is enough input to test the clinical hypothesis without making intensity the objective?

### Wednesday — REASSESS BEFORE YOU ADD MORE
**Focus: Reassessment**

A plan is provisional. Check the response before extending, intensifying, or stacking interventions. A meaningful reassessment may include the patient's report, observable function, and agreed-upon goals; it is not a claim that every sensation reveals a specific nervous-system mechanism.

**Distinctive question:** What changed, and does the next step still make sense?

### Thursday — CHANGE THE PLAN, NOT THE PATIENT
**Focus: Decision**

When an intervention is not serving the agreed purpose, clinical authority means adapting rather than persuading the patient to tolerate more. Modification, referral, or stopping may be appropriate depending on the situation and professional scope.

**Distinctive question:** Am I responding to the patient or defending my original plan?

### Friday — KNOW WHEN THE WORK IS COMPLETE
**Focus: Completion**

More treatment is not automatically more care. Close the encounter when its useful purpose has been met, the response suggests a different course, or further intervention lacks a clear rationale. Completion includes communicating what was observed without claiming ownership of the outcome.

**Distinctive question:** What justifies continuing—and what would justify ending here?

## Editorial Guardrails

- Do not equate restraint with passivity, undertreatment, or a universal preference for lighter pressure.
- Do not imply that a clinician can directly read nervous-system states from tissue feel or behavior.
- Do not claim that a particular dose, pause, or phrase reliably produces regulation or a predictable outcome.
- Do not replace individualized assessment, consent, scope of practice, or referral judgment with an aphorism.
- Maintain Drew's experienced, practical clinical voice. Prefer observable decisions and treatment-room specificity over repetitive short-form maxims.
- Use the Tao / Wu Wei connection as an interpretive frame, not a substitute for clinical reasoning.
- Keep each day's argument distinct; do not publish five variations of “less is more.”

## Book Alignment

The Tao's treatment of non-imposition, matching rather than imposing pressure, and waiting as a clinical skill informs the issue. Issue 015 extends those ideas into a decision sequence: **why intervene, how much, what changed, what next, and when finished**. The long-form article should translate the book's philosophy into specific clinical reasoning without reproducing its passages or presenting speculative neuroscience as established fact.
