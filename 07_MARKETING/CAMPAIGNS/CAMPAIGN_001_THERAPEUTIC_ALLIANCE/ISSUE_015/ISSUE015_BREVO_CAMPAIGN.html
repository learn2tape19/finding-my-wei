Knowing when to put our hands down is part of knowing how to use them.
The Tao of Clinical Touch
ISSUE 015 · RESTRAINT

There is a moment in almost every treatment when the clinician has a choice.

The technique is working—or at least something is happening. The patient has given us information. We could continue, increase the pressure, move to the next structure, or introduce another approach. We could also stop long enough to ask whether the next intervention has earned its place.

Experience does not eliminate that decision. It makes us more accountable for it.

But the person on the table did not come to experience the full range of our skills. They came with a concern, a goal, and a nervous system responding to the encounter in real time.

RESTRAINT.

Clinical restraint begins there. Not with the instruction to do less, but with the discipline to make each intervention answer to a purpose.

That is the idea at the center of this week’s The Tao of Clinical Touch:

Indication → Calibration → Reassessment → Decision → Completion

A clear indication does not guarantee a result. It gives us something more valuable: a reason to begin and a basis for deciding whether to continue.

When I match the intervention to the response rather than asking the response to justify my preferred intervention, restraint becomes a form of precision.

But a change is information, not an instruction.

If the intervention is not serving its purpose, changing course is not a failure of confidence. It may be the clearest expression of it.

A session has a scheduled ending. Useful intervention may have an earlier one.

This is not an argument for shorter sessions, lighter treatment, or doing the minimum. It is an argument against mistaking more intervention for more care.

Knowing why to choose one, when to change it, and when to put our hands down is what makes those options useful.
