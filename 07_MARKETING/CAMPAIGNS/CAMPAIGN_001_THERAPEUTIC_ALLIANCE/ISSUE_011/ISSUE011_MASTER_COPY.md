## Monday — RESPONSE BEGINS WITH WHAT CHANGED

### Feed visual copy

RESPONSE BEGINS WITH WHAT CHANGED

The next decision should belong to the person in front of us now, not the person who arrived twenty minutes ago.

Notice first. Respond second.

### Canonical feed caption

We assess.

We form a hypothesis.

We choose an approach.

And then we begin.

But something important happens once the encounter is underway:

The person changes.

Maybe the movement that felt guarded ten minutes ago feels easier now.

Maybe the pressure they initially preferred no longer feels useful.

Maybe their breathing changes.

Maybe they become quieter.

Maybe something they couldn't describe at the beginning becomes easier to recognize.

The original assessment gave us useful information.

It did not give us permanent information.

That's an important distinction.

Because it's remarkably easy to keep treating the person who walked through the door instead of the person who is in front of us now.

Clinical reasoning doesn't stop when treatment begins.

Treatment itself generates information.

The question is whether we're still available to notice it.

Before deciding what comes next, ask:

What changed?

Then let the answer matter.

Notice first. Respond second.

## Tuesday — NOT EVERY RESPONSE REQUIRES MORE

### Feed visual copy

NOT EVERY RESPONSE REQUIRES MORE

More pressure, more technique, and more time are not automatically better responses.

Sometimes the most useful adjustment is less.

### Canonical feed caption

There's a particular temptation that shows up when something isn't changing the way we expected.

Do more.

A little more pressure.

Another technique.

Another position.

Another explanation.

Another five minutes.

Doing something can feel reassuring because it feels like we're responding.

But activity and responsiveness aren't the same thing.

Sometimes the information in front of us is asking for less.

Less pressure.

Less complexity.

Less urgency.

Less talking.

Less insistence that something needs to change before the session can be considered useful.

And sometimes the appropriate response is simply to wait long enough to see what happens.

This isn't passivity.

It's restraint.

The ability to reduce an intervention is just as much a clinical skill as knowing how to add one.

We don't demonstrate our value by filling every available minute with technique.

We demonstrate it by allowing what we're learning to influence what we do next.

Sometimes that means more.

Sometimes it means less.

## Wednesday — LET THE RESPONSE MATCH THE INFORMATION

### Feed visual copy

LET THE RESPONSE MATCH THE INFORMATION

A small change in presentation doesn't always require a large change in plan.

Respond to what you know. Not everything you can imagine.

### Canonical feed caption

A person moves differently after an intervention.

What does that mean?

Maybe something important changed.

Maybe they're less guarded.

Maybe they're more confident.

Maybe the position is simply more familiar the second time.

Maybe they're trying harder.

Maybe it's ordinary variability.

The observation is real.

Our explanation is still an explanation.

This is one of the places clinical reasoning can get ahead of itself.

We notice something.

We interpret it.

Then, without realizing it, the interpretation becomes a conclusion.

One finding can suddenly reorganize an entire treatment plan.

But the size of our response should match the strength of the information.

A small observation may justify a small adjustment.

A repeated pattern may justify more.

And sometimes the appropriate response is to remain curious long enough to gather another piece of information.

Responsiveness doesn't require abandoning the plan every time something changes.

It requires allowing the plan to remain adjustable.

Change only what the information supports.

## Thursday — A RESPONSE CAN BE A QUESTION

### Feed visual copy

A RESPONSE CAN BE A QUESTION

We don't have to convert every observation into an immediate action.

Curiosity is a clinical response.

### Canonical feed caption

Something changes.

And almost immediately, the clinician's mind wants to explain it.

Why did that happen?

What structure did I affect?

What should I do next?

Those aren't unreasonable questions.

But there's another person in the room who may have useful information.

The person experiencing it.

What did you notice?

Does that feel different?

Different how?

Is that change useful to you?

Would you like to stay here, move on, or try something else?

A question does something another technique cannot:

It gives the person an opportunity to contribute information we don't have.

We don't need to turn every observation into an intervention.

Sometimes our next clinical action can simply be curiosity.

Ask.

Listen.

Then decide whether anything actually needs to happen next.

A response can be a question.

## Friday — RESPONSE COMPLETES THE LOOP

### Feed visual copy

RESPONSE COMPLETES THE LOOP

Clinical reasoning isn't finished when we notice something. It becomes useful when what we notice changes what happens next.

Listen. Respond. Listen again.

### Canonical feed caption

Over the past seven issues, we've been building something.

Understanding.

Before deciding what to do, understand what you're encountering.

Preparation.

Create conditions that make useful information easier to receive.

Permission.

Allow the person to remain an active participant in what happens.

Listening.

Notice what they tell you—with words, movement, behavior, preference, and response.

Timing.

Recognize that when we act can matter as much as what we do.

Possibility.

Leave enough room for the encounter to show us something we didn't predict.

And now:

Response.

Allow what you've learned to change what happens next.

But response isn't the end of the sequence.

Because the moment we respond, something else happens.

We create new information.

The person responds to our response.

And we're listening again.

That's why clinical reasoning isn't a straight line from assessment to treatment to outcome.

It's a loop.

Understand. Prepare. Ask. Listen. Time. Allow. Respond.

Then listen again.

Response completes the loop.
