## Body

We assess.

We form a hypothesis.

We make a plan.

And then something inconvenient happens.

The person changes.

Maybe movement becomes easier.

Maybe the pressure they initially preferred stops feeling useful.

Maybe they become more comfortable.

Maybe something they couldn't describe at the beginning of the session becomes clearer twenty minutes later.

Whatever changed, we now have information we didn't have when we made the plan.

And that raises a question:

Are we treating the person in front of us now—or the person who walked through the door?

I've been thinking about that question as I worked on this week's issue of The Tao of Clinical Touch.

Clinical reasoning is often described as the process that helps us decide what to do.

I think that's only half of it.

Because once we do something, the person responds.

And their response becomes the next piece of information.

Sometimes it tells us to continue.

Sometimes to change direction.

Sometimes to do less.

And sometimes it tells us we don't have enough information yet.

That's where a question may be more useful than another technique.

What changed?

What did you notice?

Does that feel useful?

What would you like to do next?

Responsiveness isn't about abandoning the plan every time something changes.

It's about refusing to become so attached to the plan that new information no longer matters.

This week I'm exploring the final piece in the progression we've been building:

Understanding → Preparation → Permission → Listening → Timing → Possibility → Response

Except I'm beginning to think it isn't a progression at all.

It may be a loop.

Because once we respond, we create new information.

And then we listen again.

**CTA:** Read — The Clinical Practice of Response

The goal isn't to make the right decision once.

It's to remain available to the information that tells us what the next decision should be.
