---

We like plans.

They give shape to uncertainty.

A person arrives. We listen to what brought them in. We observe. We assess. We form a hypothesis about what might be useful.

Then we begin.

That process matters.

But there's a problem if we think clinical reasoning ends there.

Because the moment we begin doing something, we create new information.

The person responds.

And suddenly the person in front of us may not be exactly the same person we assessed at the beginning of the session.

### The Plan Is Only the Beginning

I don't think good clinical practice requires us to arrive without a plan.

Quite the opposite.

Preparation matters.

A hypothesis matters.

Experience matters.

The mistake is treating any of those things as a contract with the future.

A plan is built from the information available when we make it.

Then the encounter begins.

Pressure is applied.

Movement is explored.

A position changes.

A question is asked.

Something feels easier.

Something becomes uncomfortable.

The person becomes more confident.

Or less certain.

Their breathing changes.

Their language changes.

Their expectation changes.

Every one of those things can become new information.

The question isn't whether our original plan was good.

The question is whether we're willing to let new information influence it.

### What Changed?

There's a question I think deserves a more regular place in clinical practice:

What changed?

Not:

Did my technique work?

Not:

Was my assessment correct?

Not even:

Is their range of motion better?

Just:

What changed?

That question is deliberately wider.

Maybe movement changed.

Maybe discomfort changed.

Maybe confidence changed.

Maybe the person understands the sensation differently.

Maybe nothing measurable changed, but they're less apprehensive about moving.

Maybe the pressure that initially felt useful now feels excessive.

Maybe something we expected to change didn't.

All of that is information.

And it creates an interesting clinical responsibility.

We have to decide whether what we just learned should change what we do next.

### Not Every Response Requires More

This is where another habit can appear.

When something isn't changing, we do more.

More pressure.

More technique.

More time.

More explanation.

More specificity.

I understand the impulse.

Doing something feels active.

And when someone has trusted us with their time, body, and money, activity can easily become confused with value.

But responsiveness isn't measured by how much we do.

Sometimes new information suggests that we should add something.

Sometimes it suggests we should change direction.

Sometimes it suggests we should reduce what we're doing.

And sometimes the most useful response is to stop interfering long enough to find out what happens next.

That requires a kind of restraint that isn't always celebrated in hands-on care.

Knowing how to do something is a skill.

Knowing when not to do it is also a skill.

### Don't Turn Every Signal Into a Story

There's another side to responsiveness.

We can respond too quickly.

A person moves differently after an intervention and our minds immediately begin constructing an explanation.

We changed the tissue.

We reduced guarding.

We improved mobility.

We found the problem.

Maybe.

Or maybe the movement was simply different the second time.

Maybe the person understood the instruction differently.

Maybe they felt safer.

Maybe they tried harder.

Maybe the change is real and meaningful but our explanation for it isn't.

Observation and interpretation are not the same thing.

That's why I think the magnitude of our response should match the strength of the information.

A small change may justify a small adjustment.

A repeated pattern may give us greater confidence.

Several pieces of information pointing in the same direction may justify changing the plan more substantially.

But one observation doesn't have to become an entire story.

Respond to what you know. Not everything you can imagine.

### A Response Can Be a Question

One of the easiest things to forget in clinical reasoning is that we aren't the only source of information in the room.

Something changes.

Before deciding what it means, we can ask.

What did you notice?

Does that feel different?

Different how?

Is that useful?

Would you like to continue?

Those aren't pauses in the treatment.

They are part of the treatment.

A question allows another nervous system, another history, and another perspective to enter the reasoning process.

It also gives us something we could never generate entirely through observation:

the person's experience of what just happened.

We don't have to convert every piece of information into another technique.

Sometimes the appropriate clinical response is curiosity.

### Response Completes the Loop

For the past several weeks, I've been exploring a progression:

Understanding → Preparation → Permission → Listening → Timing → Possibility → Response

At first glance, that looks like a sequence.

I'm beginning to think it's a loop.

Understanding helps us prepare.

Preparation creates room for permission.

Permission allows us to listen.

Listening changes our timing.

Timing opens possibility.

Possibility gives us more than one way to respond.

And our response creates new information.

So we listen again.

Maybe that's a more useful way to think about clinical reasoning.

Not as the process that helps us discover the correct intervention.

But as the process that keeps us responsive while the encounter changes.

We don't have to make the right decision once.

We have to remain available to the information that tells us what the next decision should be.

Response begins when new information is allowed to matter.
